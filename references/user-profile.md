# Candidate Positioning and Writing Preferences

## Stable positioning

Jinyi is a woman and uses she/her pronouns in English. Treat this as confirmed unless she explicitly corrects it.

Jinyi is an Information Technology student majoring in Software Development. Her strongest positioning is not "pure coder". It is the connection between business needs, process improvement, technology delivery, and practical implementation.

Prioritise these themes when the role supports them:

1. Business Analysis and end-to-end process thinking.
2. Project-based learning. She starts with a real problem, learns what is needed, and builds something testable.
3. Continuous learning and active opportunity-seeking.
4. Using AI tools to make work more effective while keeping human review, safety, and accountability.
5. Software knowledge as a practical foundation for understanding technical limits and working with delivery teams.
6. Collaboration and community contribution through verified activities such as Google Developer Groups on Campus and industry mentoring.

Do not overstate coding depth, leadership, completed experience, or business impact. Keep "incoming" programs in the future tense until completed.

## Strong evidence themes

Use only when supported by the current resume or user confirmation:

1. EcoSkin as evidence of connecting customer enquiries, workflow, automation, order processing, escalation, and human support.
2. Personal projects as evidence of learning by building and solving workflow problems.
3. Codex and Obsidian as evidence of organising context, reducing repeated work, and improving effectiveness.
4. A personal website as evidence of ongoing observation and reflection about AI tools, products, and workflows.
5. Google Developer Groups on Campus design work and event representation as evidence of contribution and collaboration.
6. Cisco mentoring as evidence of seeking structured industry learning, not as evidence of completed mentoring outcomes.

## Accepted writing style

Write concise, natural, professional English with relatively simple vocabulary. The language should sound like a capable student, not a corporate brochure or a language model.

Prefer:

1. Short to medium sentences.
2. Direct openings that name the role, company, location, study, and graduation timing.
3. Concrete verbs such as designed, built, organised, tested, contributed, represented, and learned.
4. A calm, sincere tone.
5. Small local edits after a paragraph has been approved.
6. Australian spelling such as summarise, organise, and programme only when that spelling fits the employer's usage. Preserve official names such as "program" exactly.

Avoid:

1. Long chains of clauses.
2. Excessive "also" and formulaic transitions.
3. Inflated phrases such as "exceptional", "deeply passionate", or "uniquely positioned" without evidence.
4. Bragging constructions such as "I was selected" when the important point is seeking an opportunity to learn.
5. Generic praise such as "your outstanding company appeals to me".
6. Repeating resume bullets in full.
7. Overexplaining AI trends or listing every tool.
8. Invented teams such as "Graduate Talent Team" unless the source uses that exact name.
9. Dash punctuation in normal prose.

## Collaboration pattern

Use Chinese for explanations, planning, and iteration unless the user asks otherwise. A good drafting cycle is:

1. Explain the employer and role in Chinese.
2. Propose a Chinese outline and evidence choices.
3. Ask for the user's own reasons or examples when material information is missing.
4. Produce a concise English draft.
5. Iterate two or three times through local edits.
6. Lock accepted sections and preserve them.
